Identifying the Potential Ecological Implications of Mining-Related Fluvial Acidification of Clear Creek, Colorado

Author: Carden Taylor  |  Sampling Date: April 25, 2026

1. Background & Argo Tunnel Context

Clear Creek flows roughly sixty-six miles along Colorado's Front Range from near Loveland Pass to just north of Commerce City as a tributary of the South Platte River. Historic mining drainage—exemplified by the Argo Tunnel west of the Argo Gold Mill in Idaho Springs—has long introduced dissolved metals into the creek, with a 2005 Ohio State University Superfund Research Program sample downstream of the Argo Tunnel reporting $2960\text{ }\mu\text{g/L}$ copper, $7610\text{ }\mu\text{g/L}$ zinc, and $19500\text{ }\mu\text{g/L}$ manganese.

2. Sampling Sites & Hypotheses

On April 25, 2026, three pH measurements were collected at distinct sub-locations within each of three sites using litmus paper (with $\pm 0.1$ reading uncertainty):

  • Georgetown (Control): Upstream baseline near the upper tributary ($39^\circ 42' 9''\text{N}$, $105^\circ 42' 12''\text{W}$)
  • Idaho Springs (Impact): Downstream of the Argo Gold Mill / Argo Tunnel influence ($39^\circ 44' 32''\text{N}$, $105^\circ 30' 22''\text{W}$)
  • Golden (Recovery): Near the tennis courts in Golden to check downstream recovery ($39^\circ 45' 16''\text{N}$, $105^\circ 13' 36''\text{W}$)

Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): Mining-related discharge from the Argo Tunnel has no effect on Clear Creek pH near Idaho Springs ($\mu_{\text{pH Georgetown}} = \mu_{\text{pH Idaho Springs}} = \mu_{\text{pH Golden}}$).

Alternative Hypothesis ($H_A$): Mining-related discharge from the Argo Tunnel has a statistically significant effect on Clear Creek pH near Idaho Springs.

At $p = 0.05$, the one-way ANOVA ($F = 46.5$, $p \approx 2.2261 \times 10^{-4}$) supports rejecting $H_0$ in favor of $H_A$, while noting that $n = 3$ trials per site warrants caution and points to a strong statistical trend rather than proof beyond all doubt.

4. Ecological Implications & Remediation

Over 75% of native wildlife depends on riparian zones (USGS), and freshwater acidification can disrupt ecosystems across trophic levels. Root damage in most flora typically occurs below pH 5.0 (reducing nutrient uptake, biomass, and food availability), whereas trout—key freshwater bioindicators, including native Greenback Cutthroat Trout—generally require pH 6.5–8.5 and experience reproductive failure below pH 6.0 (Vermont Fish & Wildlife Department). Although observed pH remained above those critical thresholds—aided by lime neutralization at the Argo Tunnel Water Treatment Facility (ATWTF, U.S. EPA)—discharge fluctuations make ongoing monitoring and waste management essential.
